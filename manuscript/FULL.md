# THE GREY WOLF — the full edition

# Chapter 1 — The Grey Wolf

Grey Ridge sat where the road stopped and the forest started: forty roofs, one well, and the long smell of smoked meat that got into everything and stayed. It stood on the western hem of the Great Star Dou Forest, where the trees went from hunted to hunting in the length of a morning's walk, and it sent pelts and smoked boar to the county town twice a year, and the county town mostly forgot it existed in between.

Ye Cang had been waiting for this day for six years.

He was six years old, and he was also a man who had died, and he kept both counts honestly. The first count: born in the coldest winter the ridge remembered, son of Ye Shan the hunter and Zhou Xia the weaver, six years of carrying water, following trails, and learning the craft from a father who never said anything twice. The second count: a night-shift tallykeeper in a city of tall grey buildings, a man who counted crates in the dark for a living and read stories on a small bright screen between loads. He had read one story twice, all of it, to the very end, both parts, because it was the good one and he had known it. He had died as he had lived, quietly, between one count and the next. Then he had been born here, with all of it still in his head.

Both counts agreed about today. In this world, the autumn a child turned six, the crystal came up from the county, and the crystal did not lie.

---

The examiner came at mid-morning on a mule, with the crystal in a padded box strapped to his back and the patience of a man who had seen four hundred villages and would see four hundred more. The ceremony was held in the stone room that was Grey Ridge's only stone room. The whole village came, because the whole village always came, and because in Grey Ridge a soul came along once a year or so, and it was the closest thing the ridge had to a festival that was not a funeral.

There were three children of age that autumn.

Little Bao, the tanner's boy, went first. He put his hand on the crystal and pushed until his face went purple, and a hatchet rose into the air over his head — a plain woodman's hatchet, turning slowly, nothing grand about it. The crystal itself stayed dark. A soul, and no fuel to run it. The village made the sound it always made, the not-unkind one, and the examiner wrote a line in the same book he had been writing in for thirty years. Little Bao would tan, and be strong, and probably be happy.

The second child was a small girl from the far roofs, and she had nothing at all, and that was rarer and sadder, and it was over quickly.

Then Ye Cang put his hand on the crystal.

The wolf came without being called. That was the first thing the room saw. The boy did not push and did not go red. He stood still, and grey light stood up out of his skin like frost forming on a window at night, and where the light passed, his hands furred grey to the knuckle, and short claws came out over his fingertips, the length of thorns. His eyes went from brown to the flat amber of a beast looking out of a den. He was still a small six-year-old under all of it. But for a dozen breaths, nothing in that room was anything except meat, and everything in the room felt it, including the two dogs outside the door, who left.

In his mind, one silent word had come with the wolf, cold and plain, said without a sound because saying it aloud was not needed: *hunt.*

The claws went back in at the twelfth breath, on their own, because the fuel ran out. That was the second thing the room saw. The boy sat down hard, and his father's hand was already behind his neck.

The crystal, when the examiner remembered to look at it, held one grey spark. Barely lit.

"Level one," the examiner said, because saying it plainly was the job. "Innate soul power, rank one." He held the box so the village could see the spark, a single ember in grey crystal. "Innate full is ten. Ten is a legend; I have written it once, in the south, thirty years ago. Most children are two, three. One cultivates. It cultivates slowly." He looked at Ye Shan, and then at the boy, and something in his tired face moved half an inch. "A wolf is a hunter's soul. The forest has always answered that soul. That is not nothing."

"It's not," said Ye Shan.

The room did its arithmetic in the quiet, friendly, terrible manner of villages. The Ye wolf, third generation: the grandfather's, strong, and the grandfather two winters in the ground. The father's — level ten for twenty years, ringless, the road past it never once opening. And now the boy: the same grey wolf, and the smallest spark anyone present could remember.

Nobody said any of it out loud. Grey Ridge was not a cruel village. It simply counted well, being full of hunters, and everyone had the total before the examiner closed his box.

"County method," the examiner said to Ye Shan, and took out a small worn card, and did the other half of the county's job. He showed the boy, once, the plain road: the sitting, the breathing, the slow rhythm of drawing power in and letting it settle, an hour a day, every day, for years, and nothing else to it. Basic Soul Power Cultivation — the road everyone walked first, the road that turned fuel into levels, one patient step at a time. He showed it once because that was the rule, and because in thirty years nobody had ever needed it twice.

Ye Cang set it in his mind whole, the first time, and it stayed there, and the examiner, who did not miss much, looked at him for a moment longer than the job required.

---

His father said nothing on the walk home, which was itself a kind of speech. Halfway, at the game trail below the big oak, he stopped, crouched, and put two fingers in a track.

"Boar," he said. "Coming down. Last night's. How old, exactly?"

Ye Cang looked at the track, at the litter blown into it, at the edges. "Morning-old," he said. "After the wind started. Nine hours, maybe ten."

His father looked at him — that grown look, the one the boy had been getting used to receiving and giving back — and put nothing on his face at all, which was how Ye Cang knew it had landed. "Six years old," his father said, to the track, "and he reads ground like the ridge owes him rent."

At the door of the house, in the last light, his father stopped him, and put his hand on his son's head, once, the same touch he gave a good bow: to know it.

"The wolf is yours," he said. "Same as mine. Same as your grandfather's. It runs in the blood, and the blood is old, and old is not the same as strong. Strong is your own."

"I know," said Ye Cang.

His father looked at him a moment more and let it go, the manner of a man who had decided six years ago that the strange parts of his son were not to be questioned, only fed.

That evening his mother put the last of the honey in his rice and did not say the soft things, because she could see he did not need them. His father told one story, the story the boy had already half collected from aunts and uncles but had never heard from the mouth it belonged to: the wall.

"Level ten," his father said. "You climb to it on fuel, one level at a time, like everyone. And at ten, the climbing stops. The tenth level is a door, and a soul ring is the key, and a soul ring comes from a soul beast, and the beast must die by your hand or it counts for nothing. No ring, no tenth level. Not in twenty years of trying. I sat at ten for twenty of them, and hunted the hem my whole life, and my beast never came." He said it flat, without self-pity, a man reading his own ledger out loud. "Your grandfather's beast never came either. The forest decides. Until it decides for you, you walk behind me and you learn the forest, so that when it decides, you are ready to answer."

"I know," said Ye Cang again, and this time he almost said the rest of it, and did not.

---

He was almost asleep when it woke up.

No voice. That was the first fact, and he learned it before anything else, and half of him had already known it would be so, because he had read the story this world was in, twice, and he knew what hung in the dark behind the eyes of the people that story happened to.

Slots. Meters. Numbers, in parts of a hundred. A status panel — whole, cold, complete — hanging in the dark of him with the patience of a ledger. And a man who had counted for a living knew a ledger when he saw one.

> 「Martial Soul — Wolf · beast-type · Power Attack · ice · Mid」
> 「Level — 1 · innate 1」
> 「Slots: 1 / 1 / 1」
> 「Technique — Basic Soul Power Cultivation: 1% · the engine, passive · Low」
> 「Technique — The Hunter's Craft: 11% · parked, unslotted · Low」
> 「Skill — The Wolf: 1% · possession · strength, speed, senses, claws · Mid」
> 「Bloodline — Grey Wolf: 7% · ice · the body-line: vitality, recovery, the wolf's frame · Low」
> 「Hunter's Sense: 13%」
> 「Plain Speech: 21%」
> 「Mountain Stride: 9%」
> 「The Tally: 27%」

He read it twice, line by line, cold as the panel itself, and made himself understand each line before he let himself feel it.

The wolf, counted at its first release: one part in a hundred. The level, one, as the crystal had said. The slots, one in every list — a technique seat, a ring seat, a bone seat, all empty but the first.

The techniques, then. The county's plain method, at one part: honest, because this body had never cultivated an hour. Under it, a line he had not been shown and had earned all his life: The Hunter's Craft, eleven parts. Six years of walking behind his father through the hem — the tracks, the wind, the knife under the crown, the waiting — counted by whatever this thing was, and never erased. Nothing in him had started at zero. Everything had started where his lives had put it.

And everything that could be graded carried its grade, cold as a market tag. The soul: Mid — ordinary, the county's own word for it. The method: Low. The craft: Low. The blood: Low, an old thin line's honest reading. The ledger did not flatter. It only weighed.

The skill: The Wolf, one part. The release itself — the grey light, the fur, the claws, the amber eyes — held up as a thing with a name and a number, counted like everything else. And under the skill, the line that made him lie very still in the dark and look at it for a long time.

Bloodline — Grey Wolf: seven parts.

A bloodline. A list none of the stories had, because none of the stories had this: the blood itself, counted. The wolf ran in the blood — his father's blood, his grandfather's, an old, thin line of it, seven parts deep and his to grow. It fed the body, he understood, slowly, reading the line's cold weight with six years of this life and thirty-one of the other. The blood was why the soul was a wolf at all. The skill was the wolf used. The bloodline was the wolf kept.

And the line had a nature, the panel said — ledgers note what things are made of, and this one noted: ice. Thin ice — the ordinary wolf's attribute, old and faint in a thin line. It explained a thing he had never thought to explain: the Ye men hunted the deep cold every winter without shivering, and had for as long as the ridge remembered them. The cold was not kindness to them. It was inheritance.

Then the life list, and he almost laughed alone in the loft, in the dark, at the bottom of it.

Hunter's Sense, thirteen — six years of woodcraft, honestly counted. Plain Speech, twenty-one — a grown man's plain talk in a child's mouth, six years of the ridge's own tongue. Mountain Stride, nine — the boy's legs, not the man's. And at the bottom, higher than everything: The Tally, twenty-seven parts. A night-shift tallykeeper's whole adult life, carried across death whole, and counted where it stood. He had been better at counting than anyone he had ever known, and whatever this thing was, it knew.

No line for reading. He could not read this world's script yet. The line would surface when the skill existed. Everything surfaced when it existed.

He ran the checks, because he had read a thousand of these stories and knew the variants. No voice: it had nothing to say. No shop, no points, no quests: it offered nothing, rewarded nothing, wanted nothing. It was not watching him. It was not anything. It was there, like the floor, and it counted. Everything else — the doing, the choosing, the living — was his.

So he made the first choice, and it took no time at all, because it was the same choice he had been quietly making for six years. One technique seat. One technique worth the seat. The county's plain method went in, and the seat closed around it like a hand around a knife handle, and the method began to run.

Not an hour a day. All the hours. Awake and asleep, breathing or not caring to, the engine turned over in the dark of him and started its slow, patient, endless pull. He lay there and felt it work — faint, far, like water moving under ice — and thought, with the professional calm of a man who had watched freight move all night for nine years: that is a machine that never stops.

Basic Soul Power Cultivation, passive. Level one, going somewhere, for the first time in the Ye line in twenty years.

---

He lay in the loft and counted what he knew, because counting was how he slept now.

He knew where he was: the Douluo continent, in the years before the story started. He knew the story whole, having read it twice and died once. Far east, in a mansion with a duke's title on the gate, there was a boy his own age with spirit eyes and a sick mother, and neither of them knew yet what the forest was going to give him. Five winters from now, that boy would walk into the trees alone, and the book this world lived inside would open its first page.

Five winters. And on this side of the continent, in a village no book had ever written down, a level-one wolf was going to spend them quietly. An engine running all night, every night. A craft behind a father through the hem, growing. A bloodline, seven parts deep, to be grown. A wall at ten that his family had stopped at for two generations, and a forest full of keys.

He knew what the forest held, and what it was already beginning to hold. He knew what rose in the east on engines of engraved metal, and what it would want, and when. He knew there was a city ten thousand years old with an academy in it that judged children once a year and judged them honestly, and he knew what that academy would ask of the ones it took. He knew the ending of the book. None of it needed him to hurry. All of it needed him to be ready, and ready was a thing you could count toward, one hour at a time, all night, every night, with the patience of a man who had moved freight through the dark for nine years and never once dropped a crate.

Outside, the ridge went quiet in its old order: dogs first, then the wind, then the forest, breathing.

At dawn, before his eyes opened, he looked.

> 「Technique — Basic Soul Power Cultivation: 2%」

Two parts. One night. The engine, running.

He was six years old, with a wolf, a ledger, and five winters, and every count he owned was moving. He slept like a man whose tally had come out even.

# Chapter 2 — The Quiet Climb

Winter came up the ridge in the old order: the dogs first, then the wind, then the silence with teeth in it.

Ye Cang met it standing in snow behind the house with his shirt hung on the fence, comfortable, watching his own breath make ghosts. His father, carrying firewood past him, looked at the shirt, and then at the boy, and then at the trees, and went inside, which was how he said everything he had decided not to say. The cold had not bitten the Ye men for as long as the ridge remembered them. The panel in the dark behind the boy's eyes had a line for that now, and the line said ice, and the line was growing.

The winter had one job in it, and the job was the wolf.

Behind the house at first light, most mornings, the grey light stood up out of his skin and the wolf came out to stay a while. His father stood at the door with his arms crossed and counted breaths out loud, because a hunter measures everything he means to keep.

"Twelve," he said, on the first snow. "Same as the stone room."

By the deep snow it was thirty. By thaw, sixty. The pool under the boy's ribs was deepening as a well deepens — no faster than the ground allows, but every day, and never once draining out overnight as it had in the stone room. The fuel cost of the wolf had not changed. The fuel supply had. That was the whole of the winter's arithmetic, and the tallykeeper in the boy approved of it as he had once approved of a loading dock that never jammed: quietly, professionally, every morning.

The village dogs worked out their arrangement with the wolf in the first week. The yard was his when the grey light was on him, and theirs the rest of the time, and nobody discussed it. What the wolf was, from the inside, took him the whole winter to map. It was not a costume and not a shape he wore; it was the world coming closer. Snow got an age to it, and a direction, and a history he could read with his nose. He could hear the dogs' hearts at the fence, four of them, each with its own temper. He could smell morning before it happened. And under the skin the body waited like a drawn bow: strength on account, speed on loan, everything rented by the breath and priced honestly. When the fuel ran out it ran out at once, and for an hour afterward his own plain body felt like a coat with the lining torn out.

Little Shan came around to watch the morning releases, two years older and sworn to silence about them, standing on the fence with his practice bow on his back and his opinions kept behind his teeth like a man raised by hunters.

"Does it hurt?" he asked once.

"It costs," said Ye Cang. "That's different."

Little Shan thought about that for three days and came back with a nod, satisfied. It was the most either of them ever said about it.

Midwinter, the village counted its stores in Old Kuo's barn, out loud, with the headman's charcoal marks on a plank, because a village that cannot count its winter does not see the spring. Ye Cang sat on a barrel and watched the count come out wrong twice, and said nothing, and Old Kuo saw him not saying it.

"Out with it, Ye boy."

"The smoked boar is forty-one, not forty-three. The west rack went in twice."

The barn went quiet in the particular manner of men checking. It came up forty-one. Old Kuo looked at the boy a long moment, then at his own plank, then dipped the charcoal.

"The ridge will keep you fed for that head," he said, and wrote the true number.

After that the winter count had a chair in it, and the chair had a barrel under it, and if that was a kind of fame, it was exactly the kind he had chosen: useful, small, and completely unremarkable to anyone from beyond the county.

---

In the second month of winter, lying in the loft with the engine turning over in the dark of him, faint and constant as water under ice, Ye Cang felt the pool find a new shelf. There was no sound to it and no ceremony. The water simply stood higher than it had stood, and the height had a name, and the name was a number, and the number was his.

> 「Level — 4」

He lay still and did the professional arithmetic, because it was the only luxury he had ever been able to afford in either life.

The county method, as taught, was an hour a day: the examiner had said so, and every soul in every village on the continent sat their hour and rose and went to work. An hour a day, for a rank-one soul on poor food, was two levels in a year, three in a lucky one. He knew that number from the inside, because he had read this world's book twice: the rank-one boy in it, alone and unfed in a rich man's house, had climbed from one to the wall at ten in three years of exactly that hour.

His engine did not do hours. It ran while he carried water, and while he sat in the snow, and while he slept, at his best and not at his mood, because the slot held it at his best for him. The machine was small — rank one was rank one, and the county's plain method was nobody's treasure — but where the county's soul worked one hour, his worked every one of them, and it compounded, a word he had loved in the old life and loved more in this one, where nobody else had ever heard it. The honest count — and he counted everything twice — put the wall at ten inside his first year and a half. The county would call that a lie. The ledger called it arithmetic, and the ledger had never once flattered him. The years after the wall would be quiet ones. He had read the story this world lived inside, and the story had never once mentioned a boy on the western hem of the great forest, and he intended to spend the quiet years getting harder to kill.

He slept. The engine did not.

---

Spring took the road down to Anlin, and Ye Shan took the cart: a season of pelts, the winter's smoked boar, and his son on top of the load with a blanket and a knife, because the trip was the craft and the craft was his schooling.

Anlin was three streets and a wall, a market, a well, and a county hall with a slate roof, and Ye Cang liked it exactly as much as he had liked every depot he had ever worked: it was where the counts happened. The pelt-buyer counted bundles. His father counted coins. The boy sat on the cart rail and counted the counting, which was his own private version of the whole town.

At the county hall, the crystal came out of its box for him, and the examiner — older in the spring light, the same mule — read the spark and read it again.

"Level six," he said. He opened the book, found his own line from the autumn — *wolf, rank one, cultivates slowly* — and did not cross it out. He wrote under it. "Faster than my word, boy."

"The method suits me," said Ye Cang.

The examiner looked at him for the length of a breath, the tired look of a man who had seen four hundred villages and been surprised by perhaps ten of them, and wrote a second line, and closed the book. Whatever the county now believed about the Ye boy, it believed it in two careful lines and a half inch of ink, and that was exactly as much noise as Ye Cang intended to make in this world for some years.

On the way back to the cart they passed the pelt-buyer's shop, and in its window burned a small lamp with no flame in it. A stone of engraved metal, lit from somewhere inside itself, cold to look at and steady. Ye Cang looked at it for exactly one breath. He knew what it was. He had read the future of this world, and the future of this world was engraved metal burning without fire — the east was already building it, street by street, army by army, and the lamp in a pelt-buyer's window on the western edge of nowhere cost more than Grey Ridge's forty roofs. The book he had read twice started with a boy and a forest and ended past a war that these lamps were the small quiet first line of.

He let it go, and got on the cart, and said nothing, and counted bundles the whole ride home.

---

Summer was the snare lines, and the snare lines were mathematics, and the mathematics was joy.

Set the loop where the trail narrows. Anchor to what will hold. Mind the wind, because everything with a nose is reading you while you read it. His father taught as he did everything: once, plainly, with his hands. Then it was the boy's line, the boy's counts, the boy's mistakes, walked at dusk and dawn with the father two steps behind and his opinions held until asked.

In the last month of summer his father creased a boar high on the east slope — a cutting stroke, not a killing one, gone wrong as hunting goes wrong sometimes, both of them listening to the brush take the animal's noise downhill. Ye Shan nocked nothing and drew nothing.

"Your track," he said. "Your call."

The boy read the blood on the leaves. Bright, then dark; splashing, then dripping; a boar that would not lie down until it had to, and would be at its most dangerous exactly when it did. The old life had read about this. The craft had a teaching for it. He let the boar have its head start, and followed at the pace of the wounded, and the whole hot afternoon went by with the world narrowed to sign and wind, and when the boar finally stood at bay in the thorn it was standing on tired legs with its one good plan already spent.

His father's spear ended it. That was the law of the ridge: a boy reads the ground, and a man takes the weight.

On the walk home, with the light going long and orange, his father said it plainly, because he was a man who paid words out like coin and never wasted one.

"You waited."

"The ground said to."

His father walked ten steps. "Your grandfather would have liked watching you," he said, to the trees, and that was all, and it was a very great deal.

That night, in the loft, a new line had surfaced in the dark behind his eyes, quiet as everything the ledger did, and Ye Cang lay still and smiled at it. The system counted even the sitting still.

---

The ninth level came up in the night near the end of summer, like a boat coming to anchor: no sound, no ceremony, the water simply standing higher. Then the year came around to the stone room's anniversary, and the year's list printed itself in the dark, complete — as it had on the first night, and as it would on every gate he ever crossed.

> 「Stillness: 6%」
> 「Plain Speech: 23%」
> 「Mountain Stride: 15%」
> 「The Tally: 29%」

He audited it line by line, because auditing was how he slept.

The engine at twenty-two parts. The craft at nineteen, grown a full winter of snares and one long afternoon of a boar. The wolf at nine — sixty breaths of grey light most mornings, and each breath worth more than the last. The blood at twelve, and the winter's proof of it hung on the fence every morning of the cold: the shirt, and the boy under the shirt, comfortable. Two lines up in the list of the life a man lives with his mouth and his legs. One new line, counted from the thorn thicket and the sitting still.

And the level: nine, at seven years old — a season from the wall his father had stood at for twenty years. The wall was never the work; the body was. A seven-year-old frame could hold ten levels as a cup holds a flood, and a hundred-and-twenty-year beast would need a man's shoulders to take it from. The winters between now and the grey beast were for the shoulders. And the book this world lived inside would open its first page somewhere far east of here, on schedule, without him.

His mother let his sleeves out twice that year and the shoulders once, working the needle in the door light with the calm of a woman who had made up her mind about something and did not require witnesses. He was growing as the ridge's boys grew, and then a little past it — the frame filling quietly under everything, bone and meat coming on like weather. She measured him with her eyes across the whole year and said only, "Eat," and put the bigger share in his bowl, and he ate, and neither of them called the bloodline by name, and neither of them needed to.

At the pond below the house he washed the anniversary's dust off, and for one breath the boy in the water looked back at him with amber eyes, and he let the wolf look, and put it away.

At the door, his father's hand came down on his head, once, the good-bow touch.

"One year," said Ye Shan.

"One year," said Ye Cang.

Outside, the ridge went quiet in its old order. Inside, the machine ran on, all hours, at his best, compounding. Nothing in the Ye line had ever moved like this before, and none of them had ever been able to see it moving.

He could. That was the whole cheat, he had decided long ago, lying in the loft with a wolf in his blood and a ledger in the dark. Not the wolf. Not the old blood. The seeing — and the patience to spend it one honest night at a time.
