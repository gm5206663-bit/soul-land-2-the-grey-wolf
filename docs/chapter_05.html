5. Chapter 5 — The Road Begins

The water moved all winter.

Thirteen came with the first deep snow. Fourteen arrived before the thaw, quietly, in his sleep, and he learned of it at dawn from the ledger's cold line and said nothing to anyone, because there was no one to say it to and nothing to compare it to. His father had sat at ten for twenty years. The county's crystal, hung in the examiner's stone room in Anlin, could have told him what the village could not: that a boy pouring level after level through a winter was not a thing the county had a column for.

He did not go to Anlin. He had no need of a second reading of what the first reading had already told him, and the ledger's arithmetic was better than the county's anyway. The engine ran. The pool rose. The doors opened one after another, shelf by shelf, and each shelf held less water than the one before it, and the water came anyway.

The body changed under it. Not the growing of the year before, which had been the ridge's ordinary weather of bone and meat, but something with a direction in it. The grey line deepened, and the predator's frame the card had promised arrived as freight arrives, box by box across a season. The wrist that no longer knew a sprain. The back that carried the winter's wood without a word of complaint. The nose that stood at the door each morning and read the whole day out of the air before the sun had cleared the ridge. The village dogs stopped lifting their heads when he passed. He had not decided to walk more quietly. The ghost's line had decided it for him, and was being fed, and was growing, and the boy simply lived inside what grew.

The releases grew with the year. The grey came on him now for whole mornings, the amber eyes at the woodpile, the claws splitting kindling with an honesty that still startled the village children and had stopped startling the village. On the deep snow mornings he ran the ridge in the wolf's body, possession and boy breathing as one animal, and once, at the far blind, he sat the grey down in the bracken and let the whole wood go past him — and the wood, taking him for the forest's own, had gone past him at peace. A possession hour had emptied him at six. At eleven he wore the grey like the coat: let out, and let out, and still fitting.

The year fed the house as it fed him. The pelts came down the mountain on his shoulders now as often as his father's, the YE column in Old Kuo's book ran two lines some months, and in the spring the village, without one word of ceremony, stopped calling him the little one and started calling him Ye Cang.

The patience grew too, and this was stranger than the rest, because patience had seemed to him a thing a man had or did not have, like a chin. It was not. It was a limb. It lengthened. He could sit the black pools blind now through a whole short day, the spear across his knees, the mind counting musk and water and the small sounds of the bog's birds, without the fidget that a ten-year-old's body owes its blood. The body outlasted the boy in it. Something old and wolfish held the sitting the boy could not, and the ledger's cold line for it climbed, and the hunting got better in exact proportion.

The netherlight grew too. He paid for the bursts honestly, on the winter's ice, at the bog's edge, one breath at a time, and the ledger's line for it climbed. Once, when a sledge runner snapped on the slope above him, the cold light arrived before his fear did. The surge took him sideways off the road's edge, the runner's iron sliding off the shimmer of him like rain off oiled cloth. The sledge's whole winter of meat was saved, and nobody but the ledger was the wiser.

The ring aged. He had felt it turn in its seat on the night it seated, and he had half thought the turning was the settling of a new thing. It was not settling. It was work. The number under the ring's line was not the number the kill had left: a year on, the ledger carried it higher, and higher again by the leaving, and the pelt that lay beside his mother's loom had begun, faintly, on warm evenings, to smell of live wolf.

And the engine's own count crossed its hundred in the last week of summer, in his sleep, the second gate opening under him without waking him. He found it at dawn: the method he had been given by a county examiner, mastered once and reopened, now mastered deeper and opened again, the third stage of a plain road he had been told had no turns in it. The ledger's line for it settled at nine parts into the new stage, and the pool, that same week, went dark in a manner he had no other word for. Dense. The water of a deeper well.

Nineteen arrived with the first cold of autumn, and stopped.

He knew the stop. He had lived beside one for three years of his first body's counting, in his father's shoulders, and he had read this one coming for half a season, as a man reads weather. The pour slowing, shelf by shelf. The last shelves holding less and less for longer and longer. Then the flat held line of it, water against stone, one number standing still in all the world. The wall at twenty. The door the forest kept the keys to.

He was not the boy who had stood at the first wall. He had known that wall as a verdict. This one he knew as a door, and he owned a key to its making, and the making was the oldest craft his father had: find what the forest offers, stand in front of it, and be the patient kind.

"It's time," he said, at the fire, in the middle of October's first week. "The second one."

His father looked at him for a long moment, as he looked at a track before deciding what had made it.

"The skirts," his father said. "Not the deep quarter. There's a pack that runs the wind line under the south crags. Stormwind. Fast kind." He turned the spit one turn. "You can't out-sit what won't stop, boy. You know that."

"I know it."

"Then say what you'll do."

"Out-decide it," Ye Cang said. "It runs. Everything that runs has a line it has to take. I'll be standing in the line."

His father turned the spit another turn, and that was the whole of the council, and they left in the dark before the dawn, the two spears on the two shoulders, the coat's ties checked by both his mother's hands and then, at the gate, once more by his own.

The wind line under the south crags was hunted country of a different alphabet. No bogs, no blinds, no patient musk — a long shoulder of scree and bent grass, all of it moving, all of it noise, the wind coming off the crags in cold ropes. They found the pack's line in the first hour, prints laid in running pairs, the stride in them longer than the law of a walking wolf had any right to allow. His father read them at a crouch.

"Six hundred years, the big one," he said. "Maybe more. The grey's son at the head, and the pack behind his temper."

"How many?"

"Enough that we take the head, or we take nothing. A stormwind pack doesn't leave a body to come back for."

They sat the shoulder for a day and a night, and the second day the pack came through the scree at the hour when the light goes long, and the boy learned what fast truly was, at six hundred years. It was not speed. Speed he had met, in the netherlight, in the boar's first burst. This was decision, the whole animal already committed to a line through the world, the body merely arriving where the mind had gone first. The pack streamed the shoulder in a grey rope, and at the head of it ran the son of the storm, iron-dark, the wind pulling a howl off its throat that arrived before it did.

Ye Cang stood up out of the grass into the line.

He had chosen the place in the first hour of the first day: a fold in the scree where every running line through the shoulder narrowed to one. The pack's line was fixed by the crags; the crags did not move; the fold did not move; the boy stood in the fold and did not move, and the spear's point led the arriving wolf by the width the craft demanded, which is to say: by nothing a slower eye could see.

The grey met the point at full stride. The point went home at the shoulder's root, the craft's whole sentence written in one line — and the wind's own weight came down the shaft and tore the shoulder of him half open before the second spear, his father's, arrived from the flank and closed the account.

The pack poured past on both sides of the killing like water past a stone, and was gone down the shoulder, and the crags stood quiet, and his father's hand was flat on his back, and the ring was rising.

Yellow. Again. Young straw darkened a season: six hundred years and the change of it, and it came to him across the scree as the first one had come across the bog: patient, unhurried, certain of its welcome. He sat into it with his torn shoulder singing and his father's arm behind him, and the second door of his life opened, and the banked water of the long autumn poured home through it — the one level the wall had held, arriving, and atop it, the ring's own rank, the continent's oldest arithmetic paid a second time. And with the evening, the third word of his life arrived, cold and plain, set in his mind with a skill's weight:

run.

The storm's own stride. The burst that is not escape but arrival, the body going where the mind went first, his now, at the price of his pool and the cost of a breath. He sat on the scree with his shoulder in pieces and the word in his head and laughed once, short, the sound the ridge would have sworn the boy did not make, because the wolf's book now read: hunt. fade. run. — and he was eleven years old, and a Great Soul Master, and the ledger's lines were climbing off the page.

The coming home was the quiet one. His mother sewed the shoulder and fed him and said nothing at all, which was her longest speech. The pelt went to the smoke-house beam beside the first. Old Kuo, at the year's turning of his book, wrote the largest number of the Ye column to date, looked at it a while, and ruled the line closed with the flat stroke he kept for finished accounts.

The leaving was made in the weeks after, without one word of it being spoken as a decision.

His mother let the hunt coat out a last time, and then, finding nothing left to let, cut it down the middle of its cleverness and rebuilt it as a road coat. Into its hem, with a needle and a silence, she sewed what ten years of pelts had brought. He could feel the weight of the hem when he lifted the coat, and knew better than to count it in front of her. His father oiled the second spear, the one that had stood against the burned cedar through the whole of the first hunt, and wrapped its head in oiled linen, and stood it by the door without a word about it, and that was the giving.

"You'll walk the forest's hem north," his father said, the last evening, at the fire. "Never in the trees. The hem roads are old, the villages know them. A season to the city, if you're honest." He looked at the fire a while. "The Ye men stop at ten. You're past twenty. I don't know what you are. I know whose you are."

"I know whose I am," Ye Cang said.

"Then the road's just arithmetic," his father said, and banked the fire.

Little Shan was at the fence in the blue cold of the leaving morning, the practice bow on his back. Ye Cang, passing, touched two fingers to his own forehead — the hunter's acknowledgment between equals, returned at last, given away whole. Little Shan took it as the ridge takes weather, and that was everything that was ever said.

At the top of the first rise he looked back once. Smoke, forty roofs, the long grey shoulder of the ridge he could hunt blind, his mother small at the gate, his father not there, which meant his father was in the trees, which meant his father would watch the road a while from inside it. Then the road went over the rise, and the ridge went into him instead, and he walked east into the cold morning with a road coat weighted at the hem, his father's spear on his shoulder, a ring aging in its seat and another aging beside it, and the whole counted life of him folded shut like Old Kuo's book.

That night, at the first gate of the road, in the loft of a hem-village inn with the smell of other men's suppers below, he looked, and the ledger gave him the whole of it, every line, as it did at every gate he had ever crossed.

「Martial Soul — Wolf · beast-type · Power Attack · ice · Mid」

「Level — 21 · Great Soul Master · the second door open · the ring's gift」

「Slots: 3 / 3 / 3」

「Soul Ring — Ghost Wolf: 131 years · yellow · aging」

「Soul Ring — Stormwind Demon Wolf: 603 years · yellow · aging」

「Technique — Basic Soul Power Cultivation — Stage Three: 9% · the engine, passive · Mid」

「Technique — The Hunter's Craft — Stage Two: 35% · seated, the second slot · Mid」

「Skill — The Wolf: 63% · possession · strength, speed, senses, claws · Mid」

「Skill — Netherlight: 18% · the speed surge, the blow sliding off · Mid」

「Skill — Windstride: 1% · the burst that arrives · Mid」

「Bloodline — Grey Wolf: 41% · ice · the body-line: vitality, recovery, the wolf's frame · Low」

「Bloodline — Ghost Wolf: 13% · the ghost-line: the patient hunt, the quiet step, the seen-late · Low」

「Bloodline — Stormwind Demon Wolf: 1% · the storm-line: the fast kind, the wind's stride · Low」

「Hunter's Sense: 52%」

「Stillness: 37%」

「Plain Speech: 38%」

「Mountain Stride: 46%」

「The Tally: 50%」

「Spear: 35%」

He read it twice, and found it honest, and slept the sleep of a man whose tally had come out even, and in the morning the road ran on east, and he ran with it.

Somewhere far down that same turning of the world, in the same autumn, a boy his own age with eyes the color of nothing was walking alone out of a great man's back gate, toward a forest, toward a ring of his own that was a million years wide, toward the same city. The book this world lived inside was opening. Ye Cang walked the hem of it, a season ahead, and the ledger counted his road the whole way.
